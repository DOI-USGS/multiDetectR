Getting started with multiDetectR

library(multiDetectR)

The problem

A survey that looks for a target often nests its sampling: sites hold samples, samples hold replicates, and so on. Each level costs something and detects the target with some probability, and a fixed budget has to be split among the levels. multiDetectR implements the closed-form rule that solves this allocation exactly, at any depth. Function and argument names follow the equations of the accompanying manuscript, so the code and the paper read the same way.

Levels are ordered from the outermost (the reporting level, index 1) to the innermost (index d). For a three-level environmental DNA survey, level 1 might be sites, level 2 water samples within a site, and level 3 PCR replicates within a sample. Two numbers describe each level:

  • theta[i]: the probability a level-i unit carries the target given that the unit containing it does (a conditional detectability);
  • cost[i]: the marginal cost of one level-i unit, excluding the cost of what it contains.

Allocating a budget

Give allocate() the per-level detectabilities and costs, a goal, and a budget:

des <- allocate(
  theta  = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.6),   # site, sample, replicate detectabilities
  cost   = c(200, 20, 2),      # cost of a site, a sample, a replicate
  goal   = "detection",
  budget = 5000
)
des
#> <multidetect_design> depth 3, goal = detection, integer counts
#>   per-level n:     16,  4,  3
#>   detection P1:    0.9943
#>   total cost C:    4864
#>   price kappa_1:   941.4 (cost per nat at the outermost level)

The design says how many units to take at each level: here, 16 sites, 4 samples per site, and 3 replicates per sample, reaching a detection probability of 0.994.

summary() lays the design out level by level, including each level’s detection hazard and its price of a detection kappa:

summary(des)
#>   level theta cost  n    hazard      kappa
#> 1     1   0.3  200 16 0.3229214 941.405614
#> 2     2   0.5   20  4 0.6311118  41.197139
#> 3     3   0.6    2  3 0.9162907   2.182713

Reaching a required detection probability

The same rule answers the dual question: what is the cheapest design that reaches a required detection probability? Supply P_target instead of budget.

allocate(theta = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.6), cost = c(200, 20, 2),
         goal = "detection", P_target = 0.95)
#> <multidetect_design> depth 3, goal = detection, integer counts
#>   per-level n:     10,  4,  3
#>   detection P1:    0.9604
#>   total cost C:    3040
#>   price kappa_1:   941.4 (cost per nat at the outermost level)

The inner design is identical to the budget version; only the number of outermost units differs. This is the budget-invariance property: replication within the hierarchy does not depend on how much money there is.

The budget-detection frontier

Once a design is reduced to its outermost price of a detection kappa_1, the whole budget-detection relationship is a single curve, P(B) = 1 - exp(-B / kappa_1):

plot(des)

The curve is smooth and strictly concave, so returns diminish everywhere at the same proportional rate; there is no natural stopping point, and the budget or a required detection has to be chosen from outside the curve.

Two goals

A search has two nested goals. The detection goal maximizes the chance of finding the target at least once. The count goal maximizes the number of level-1 samples in which it is found. They agree when a sample seldom carries the target, and diverge as it becomes common.

allocate(theta = c(0.3, 0.5), cost = c(200, 2),
         goal = "count", budget = 5000)
#> <multidetect_design> depth 2, goal = count, integer counts
#>   per-level n:     23,  6
#>   detection P1:    0.9997
#>   total cost C:    4876
#>   price kappa_1:   605.7 (cost per nat at the outermost level)

Integer counts and small budgets

By default replicate counts are integers, obtained by rounding in place. When a budget buys only a handful of outermost units, rounding can miss the best integer design, so enumerate instead:

enumerate_designs(theta = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.6), cost = c(200, 20, 2),
                  budget = 1500, goal = "detection")
#> <multidetect_design> depth 3, goal = detection, integer counts
#>   per-level n:     5, 4, 2
#>   detection P1:    0.7870
#>   total cost C:    1480
#>   price kappa_1:   957 (cost per nat at the outermost level)

For effort measured continuously (a volume, a time, a distance), set integer = FALSE and no rounding is needed.

Auditing a design

At the optimum, each level spends on its contents the same share of its budget as those contents contribute to its detection power (the identity A = B). optimality_identity() reports both quantities, so a costed design can be checked level by level without re-solving:

optimality_identity(allocate(theta = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.6), cost = c(200, 20, 2),
                             goal = "detection", budget = 5000,
                             integer = FALSE))
#>   level         A         B         diff
#> 1     1 0.3148112 0.3148112 3.830714e-12
#> 2     2 0.2393080 0.2393080 1.347057e-10

The differences are zero (to numerical tolerance) for a continuous optimum.

Choosing among branches

Separate places or separate methods are branches, not nested levels. allocate_branches() decides whether to concentrate the budget on one branch or spread it, from the per-target, per-branch detection rates.

## One target, two methods; the first is nine times better.
allocate_branches(rate = matrix(c(0.9, 0.1), nrow = 1), budget = 20)
#> $share
#> [1] 1.000000e+00 9.204505e-12
#> 
#> $concentrate
#> [1] TRUE
#> 
#> $detection
#> [1] 1
#> 
#> $regime
#> [1] "dominance"

A method that dominates every target takes the whole budget; two places that each reach targets the other cannot are always shared.
